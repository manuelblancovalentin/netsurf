\section{Discussion and Limits}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What a designer can take from the measurements.} First, evaluate masks in the task metric at the intended BER and budget: three gradient scores with almost equal single-flip correlations (0.36 to 0.38 on ResNet-8) have worst regrets of 2.1, 0.06 and 3.2 points (Appendix~\ref{app:evidence}). Second, the per-example absolute gradient is a sound default, computed in seconds to minutes for the MLPerf Tiny models. Its worst case stays small, but it is not the best in every cell (Section~\ref{sec:reversals}). Third, significance-first protection is a fallback for when no calibration data exist, not a neutral choice: it gives up a factor of about three in tolerable BER on four of the six models and trails by 9 to 34 points on every ResNet-8 checkpoint at a moderate fault rate. Fourth, scores that select by the predicted \emph{direction} of a flip should be avoided unless faults are known to be sparse, because what they leave unprotected is one-sided, and they need hundreds of calibration inputs. Fifth, at dense fault rates a ranking pays off even against blanket correction (Section~\ref{sec:correction}).

\paragraph{Why not optimize the mask for each deployment?} For models of this size a search by fault injection, reinforcement learning or gradient-based optimization costs minutes to hours, so runtime is not the main argument for a fixed ranking. The argument is that the best mask depends on the fault rate (Sections~\ref{sec:allocation} and~\ref{sec:reversals}), which is not known when the design is committed: it varies with the radiation environment, the supply voltage, ageing and the time since the last scrub. A search answers which mask is best at the rate it assumed, not how much is lost by committing to one mask over the rates the device will meet. On the pinned ResNet-8, Monte Carlo-guided, relaxed, group-structured and Thales- and HarDNN-style allocators (Appendix~\ref{app:search}) did not beat the absolute-gradient mask by a point where the model is usable; the relaxed optimizers lost 4 to 12 points there and the Thales and HarDNN rankings 4 to 66, while the group-structured mask, which hardware can place cheaply, lost at most one. This is one checkpoint and one implementation of each method, not evidence that learned allocators are worse in general.

\paragraph{Limits.} Each model except ResNet-8 has one trained checkpoint, and the pruned and under-trained ResNet-8 variants are single training runs. Calibration draws, masks and windows were varied only for ResNet-8 and DS-CNN, several cohorts had been used before, and for ToyCar and the transformers the default claim remains exploratory. Outside Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} every mask uses 64 calibration inputs; calibration size was varied only for the ResNet-8 gradient rankings at $p\ge10^{-3}$. Counting protected bits ignores mask regularity: at 12.5\% the ResNet-8 absolute-gradient mask protects 59\% of sign bits, 28\% of the next tier and 10\% of the third, and its placement or metadata is not priced. Biases, scales, embeddings and normalization parameters are never faulted, which protects them for free, and activation, datapath and control faults are outside the fault model. Faults are independent and persistent, logical BER is a model parameter, and the band in which masks differ, about $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$, is a regime of dense memory errors that periodic scrubbing would shorten~\cite{biswas2005avf}. FKeras was not measured on the transformers, and the other allocators on one ResNet-8 checkpoint only. Gradient and Hessian scores come from differentiable companions, the DistilBERT calibration sentences were seen in fine-tuning, and token losses are capped at 30 nats, so language-model magnitudes outside the useful region are lower bounds.
