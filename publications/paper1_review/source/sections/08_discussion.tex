\section{Discussion and Limits}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What a designer can take from the measurements.} First, evaluate masks in the task metric at the intended BER and budget: the three gradient scores correlate almost equally with measured single-flip damage on ResNet-8 (Spearman 0.36 to 0.38), yet their worst regrets are 2.1, 0.06 and 3.2 points (Appendix~\ref{app:evidence}). Second, among cheap static rankings the per-example absolute gradient is a sound default; its scores take seconds to minutes for the MLPerf Tiny models. Third, significance-first protection is a fallback for when no calibration data exist, not a neutral choice: it gives up a factor of about three in tolerable BER on four of the six models, its regret on ResNet-8 ranges from 13 to 49 points across checkpoints, and one reference checkpoint can make it look much better than it typically is. Fourth, scores that select by the predicted \emph{direction} of a flip should be avoided for dense faults, because what they leave unprotected is one-sided. Fifth, at dense fault rates a ranking pays off even against blanket correction, because a triplicated bit fails only when two of its own copies flip.

\paragraph{Why not optimize the mask for each deployment?} For models of this size, searching for the best mask by fault injection, reinforcement learning or gradient-based optimization costs minutes to hours, which is small next to the cost of a hardware design, so runtime is not the main argument for a fixed ranking. The argument is that the best mask depends on the fault rate, as Section~\ref{sec:allocation} shows in principle and the under-trained checkpoint in practice, and that the fault rate is not known when the design is committed: it varies with the radiation environment, the supply voltage, ageing and the time since storage was last scrubbed. A search answers which mask is best at the fault rate it assumed, not which mask is safe over the rates the device will meet. The question a designer can act on is how much is lost by committing to one mask under an uncertain operating point, whether a fixed ranking, a mask optimized for one fault rate, or one optimized over the plausible range. Masks optimized bit by bit are also irregular, which a physical mechanism has to pay for, and for language models with billions of eligible bits such an optimization is itself expensive. Exact local re-selection of two bits in six-bit DS-CNN pools changed risk by too little to matter (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}), which does not show whether a search with a useful search space would.

\paragraph{Limits.} Each model except ResNet-8 has one trained checkpoint; for ResNet-8 five retrained copies were measured, but the pruned and under-trained variants are single training runs. Calibration draws, masks and windows were varied only for ResNet-8 and DS-CNN, several cohorts had been used before, and for ToyCar and the transformers the default claim remains exploratory. Counting protected bits treats regular and irregular masks alike: at 12.5\% on ResNet-8 the absolute-gradient mask protects 59\% of sign bits, 28\% of the next tier and 10\% of the third, and the placement or metadata this needs is not priced. Biases, scales, input embeddings and normalization parameters are never faulted, which amounts to protecting them for free, and activation, datapath and control faults are outside the fault model. Faults are independent and persistent, logical BER is a model parameter, and the band in which masks differ, about $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$, is a regime of dense memory errors that periodic scrubbing would shorten~\cite{biswas2005avf}. FKeras was not measured on the transformers and no search-based or learned allocator was measured. Gradient and Hessian scores come from differentiable companions of the integer programs, the DistilBERT calibration sentences were seen in fine-tuning, and token losses are capped at 30 nats, so language-model magnitudes outside the useful region are lower bounds.
