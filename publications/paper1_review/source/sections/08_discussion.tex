\section{Discussion and Limits}\label{sec:discussion}
The results favor condition-specific evaluation, not a blanket rejection of static rankings. A low-cost structural mask can be competitive, while a gradient-derived mask can preserve substantially more quality elsewhere. Paired reversals show that the preference between tested methods can change; overlapping marginal error bars alone would not establish this. The conditional study adds an important limit: changed allocations do not necessarily produce a resolved improvement over exact isolated measurements.

These outcomes do not isolate one mechanism. Numerical encoding, surrogate error, calibration sensitivity, nonadditive task metrics, loss clipping and multiple-fault interactions can all affect a comparison. Exact response-table diagnostics show that outside faults can change local protection preferences, but do not establish that network size or BER causes a universal transition to an interaction-dominated regime. The bounded Monte Carlo alternative is not a generally superior optimizer.

The conclusions apply to one checkpoint and bounded input cohort per model. Short language-model segments and capped high-BER losses limit generalization; Pythia lacks a separate finite-cap-frequency record. Missing transformer Hessian and matched adaptive-allocation comparisons prevent an exhaustive state-of-the-art claim. Persistent parameter faults also exclude activation, datapath and control-state corruption. Ideal suppression measures a software allocation decision, not ECC/TMR effectiveness or radiation-calibrated reliability.

For deployment, the actionable procedure is to compare affordable masks under paired faults at the intended conditions, then consider effect size, uncertainty and construction cost together. If the data do not distinguish two allocations, they provide no protection-quality justification for preferring the more expensive one.
