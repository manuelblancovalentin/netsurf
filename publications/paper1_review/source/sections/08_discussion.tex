\section{Discussion and Limits}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What a designer can take from the measurements.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Judge masks in the task metric, at the intended BER and budget, with the mechanism's own failures included. A single-bit score proposes a mask; it does not judge one: three gradient scores with almost equal single-flip correlations (0.36 to 0.38 on ResNet-8) protect very differently (\wref{Appendix}{app:single}).
\item Do not protect by bit position alone. Significance-first protection, the basis of FORTUNE and of FKeras's tier-first order, is a fallback for when no calibration data exist.
\item Rank by the per-example magnitude of the gradient effect. It mixes significance tiers, as the FKeras authors anticipated a better ranking should, is a magnitude proxy for the spread that does not deliberately leave a one-sided remainder, and it is computed in seconds to minutes for the MLPerf Tiny models.
\item Treat rankings that use the direction of a flip as specific to a fault rate, and when the rate is uncertain design for its dense end.
\item Choose the mechanism with the ranking and for the model: codes where faults are rare or every weight matters, triplication of ranked bits where faults are dense, checksum correction only where faults are very rare (\wref{Figs.}{fig:mechanism} and~\ref{fig:mechanalytic}). These are storage-only comparisons of abstract mechanisms; whether the noise-plus-events surrogate can rank mechanisms without fault injection, given that codes make residual errors dependent within a codeword, is untested.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why not optimize the mask for each deployment?} For models of this size a search by fault injection, reinforcement learning or gradient-based optimization is affordable, so runtime is not the main argument for a fixed ranking. The argument is that the best mask depends on a fault rate that is not known when the design is committed. Where we did optimize, on the reference ResNet-8 and on DS-CNN, Monte Carlo-guided, relaxed, group-structured and Thales- and HarDNN-style allocators did not beat the absolute-gradient mask by a point where the model is usable, and swaps that looked best on 32 fault fields lost their predicted gain on new fields (\wref{Appendices}{app:search} and~\ref{app:ratechoice}). Stochastic and learned allocators remain promising, and the statistical model gives them an alternative objective, at the cost of one network pass per draw; these are single implementations on one or two models, not evidence that learned allocators are worse in general.

\paragraph{Limits.} Each architecture except ResNet-8 has one training run per checkpoint, and each language model has one cohort and 16 fault fields. The statistical model was checked on 18 TFLite checkpoints of four architectures, not on the language models, and against measurements that already existed when its pass criteria were written. Some evaluation windows had been used in other analyses of the same models, though never to build a mask. Protection cost is counted in stored bits; mask regularity, voters, encoders and decoders are not priced. Biases, scales, embeddings and normalization parameters are never faulted, and activation, datapath and control faults are outside the fault model. Faults are independent and persistent, logical BER is a model parameter, and the band in which masks differ, about $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$, is a regime of dense memory errors that periodic scrubbing would shorten~\cite{biswas2005avf}. Gradient and Hessian scores come from differentiable companions, and language-model losses are capped at 30 nats per token.

\paragraph{Next steps.} The statistical model suggests an allocator that chooses, for each bit or word, no protection, triplication, five-fold redundancy or a code, from each mechanism's residual error and a few network passes. Pricing those choices in area and power and calibrating the fault rate to a radiation environment belong to a hardware study that this work prepares.
