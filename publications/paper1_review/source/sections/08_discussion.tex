\section{Discussion and Limits}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What a designer can take from the measurements.} First, evaluate masks in the task metric at the intended BER and budget. Single-fault fidelity did not predict protection quality: the three gradient scores correlate almost equally with measured single-flip damage on ResNet-8 (Spearman 0.36 to 0.38), yet their worst regrets are 2.1, 0.06 and 3.2 points (Appendix~\ref{app:evidence}). Second, among cheap static rankings the per-example absolute gradient is a sound default on the measured models; its scores take seconds to minutes for the MLPerf Tiny models. Third, significance-first protection is a fallback for when no calibration data exist, not a neutral choice: it already gives up a factor of about three in tolerable BER on four of the six models, and on pruned and under-trained ResNet-8 checkpoints its regret grows to 19 to 49 points. Fourth, scores that select by the predicted \emph{direction} of a flip should be avoided for dense faults, because what they leave unprotected is one-sided; on a checkpoint far from a loss minimum the same property briefly helps and then fails. Fifth, at dense fault rates a ranking pays off even against blanket correction, because triplicating ranked bits fails only when two copies of the same bit flip.

\paragraph{Why not optimize the mask for each deployment?} For models of the size studied here, searching for the best mask by fault injection, reinforcement learning or gradient-based optimization costs minutes to hours, which is small next to the cost of a hardware design. Runtime is therefore not the main argument for a fixed ranking. The argument is that the best mask depends on the fault rate, as Section~\ref{sec:allocation} shows in principle and the under-trained checkpoint shows in practice, and that the fault rate is not known when the design is committed: it varies with the radiation environment, the supply voltage, ageing and the time since storage was last scrubbed. A search answers which mask is best at the fault rate it assumed, not which mask is safe over the rates the device will meet. The question a designer can act on is how much is lost by committing to one mask when the operating point is uncertain,: a fixed ranking, a mask optimized for one fault rate, or one optimized over the whole plausible range. Two further considerations favour a cheap fixed ranking where it is close to the best: masks optimized bit by bit are irregular, which a physical mechanism has to pay for, and for language models with billions of eligible bits an optimization over every bit is itself expensive. Exact local re-selection of two bits in six-bit pools of DS-CNN changed risk by too little to matter (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}); that study does not show whether a search with a useful search space would.

\paragraph{Limits.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Checkpoints and repetitions.} Each model except ResNet-8 has a single trained checkpoint, and the ResNet-8 variants are single training runs. Calibration draws, masks and windows were varied only for ResNet-8 and DS-CNN, and several cohorts, including the DS-CNN windows, had been used before; for ToyCar and the transformers the default claim remains exploratory.
\item \emph{Bit-count cost.} Counting protected bits treats regular and irregular masks alike. At 12.5\% on ResNet-8 the absolute-gradient mask protects 59\% of sign bits, 28\% of the next tier and 10\% of the third, covering 6\% to 63\% of individual tensors; the placement or metadata this needs is not priced.
\item \emph{Excluded state.} Biases, scales, input embeddings and normalization parameters are never faulted, which amounts to protecting them for free. Activation, datapath and control faults are outside the fault model.
\item \emph{Fault regime.} Faults are independent and persistent, and logical BER is a model parameter. The band in which masks differ lies between about $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-1}$, a regime of dense memory errors rather than rare single upsets; periodic scrubbing would bound the accumulation and lower the effective $p$~\cite{biswas2005avf}.
\item \emph{Comparators and surrogates.} FKeras was not measured on the transformers, and no search-based or learned allocator was measured. Gradient and Hessian scores come from differentiable companions of the integer programs, and the DistilBERT calibration sentences were seen in fine-tuning. Token losses are capped at 30 nats, so language-model magnitudes outside the useful region are lower bounds.
\end{itemize}
