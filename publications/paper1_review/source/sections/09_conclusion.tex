\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Choosing which stored bits of a quantized network to protect is neither a single-bit problem nor an intractable one. Under a protection mask, stored-bit faults can be replaced by Gaussian noise on every weight, set by the exact moments of its unprotected bits, plus discrete events in the top-order bits; passed through the network, this moment-matched surrogate reproduced measured accuracy within half a point, averaged per checkpoint, and which of two masks is better in 437 of 443 cases, on 18 TFLite checkpoints it was not developed on, although its Gaussian has the shape of the low-bit disturbance only at dense fault rates. Through its drift and spread it gives a reading of what we measured on 24 checkpoints from 4,000 to 600 million weights with every eligible weight bit and its redundancy allowed to fail. Ranking bits by the per-example magnitude of their gradient effect, which mixes significance tiers, beats ranking by bit position, as FORTUNE does, and by FKeras's tier-first Hessian order: it protected significantly better in 222 of the conditions tested and significantly worse in none where a model is still usable. No static ranking is guaranteed: rankings that use the sign of a flip help only at sparse faults on some models and can be worse than no protection. Mechanisms are relative in the same way: ranked triplication and codes each have models and fault rates where they are the right choice, checksum correction rarely does, and the comparison that decides between them took from minutes for the smallest models to a few hundred CPU core-hours for the largest (Appendix~\ref{app:designs}).
