\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We compared static bit rankings as equal-budget protection masks on six frozen INT8 models and, for the choice between rankings, on ten further checkpoints. Where the model is still useful, the ranking decides how far the fault rate can rise. The per-example absolute gradient is a strong default: with 64 calibration inputs it holds across masks, inputs, pruned checkpoints and retrained copies, no tested search or literature-derived allocator beats it on ResNet-8, and with up to 1,024 calibration inputs it trails no gradient ranking there by more than 2.5 points where the model is usable in the replay of Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}. No fixed ranking is best everywhere, but the cases differ. Significance-first protection falls far behind wherever faults are dense, on every model measured. On ResNet-8 the gradient rankings switch order with the fault rate at a BER that can be predicted from calibration data, and choosing the mask by the expected rate is never far behind, whereas each fixed gradient ranking loses on one side of the switch. On DS-CNN and on ten checkpoints of three further architectures no such switch appears and the absolute gradient is not beaten; we found no fault-free check that tells the two kinds of model apart, so a short fault-injection run at the expected rate is the test to make. Protecting only loss-increasing flips can be worse than none. At dense fault rates, triplicating ranked bits outperforms blanket codes and checksum correction of equal storage. All results assume ideal logical protection.
